01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

A camera usually records time-averaged intensity, not the complex amplitude U = A eiφ. Many fields therefore share the same camera image. A constant phase rotation changes U but not |U|². For a thin transparent sample, the phase delay is approximately φ = (2π/λ₀)Δn h, linking phase to optical thickness rather than absorption.

Holography introduces a known coherent reference R. Its interference with U makes the measurement sensitive to their relative phase. Expanding the measured intensity is the essential first step: it tells us which parts of the data contain the desired field.

|U + R|² = |U|² + |R|² + UR* + U*R

An untilted reference leaves these terms overlapping in frequency space. A tilted reference R = Arei2πf_cx shifts the two cross terms in opposite directions. The Fourier transform of the real hologram then contains a central background and two conjugate sidebands. Isolating one sideband can recover a complex field, even though the camera recorded only a real intensity.

The simulated object on this page is explicitly band-limited to radius B = 8 Fourier bins. This makes the sampling conditions testable rather than merely visual. The known reference fixes amplitude and phase conventions, so the displayed reconstruction error can be compared directly with the input field.

By the end, you can

  1. Expand a hologram into its background and interference terms.
  2. Choose a carrier and Fourier crop that isolate the desired field.
  3. Distinguish reference-based recovery from iterative phase retrieval and its ambiguities.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

Separation and sampling have to hold together.

If U has bandwidth B, its intensity |U|² can extend to 2B. The sideband centred at f_c occupies approximately f_c ± B along the carrier direction. To separate it from the central term, use f_c > 3B. To keep it inside the sampled band, also require f_c + B < f_Nyquist. A real object with spectral tails needs margins rather than relying on exact compact support.

The reconstruction here selects the negative-carrier term UR*, translates it to zero frequency, takes an inverse FFT, and divides by the reference amplitude. Selecting the other island gives the conjugate field unless the convention is adjusted. An arbitrary global phase remains unobservable if the reference phase itself is unknown. Once a field is recovered, the propagation operator from E01 can numerically refocus it.

Without a reference, constraints do the work.

Phase retrieval starts from measurements such as a Fourier magnitude and assumptions such as a known spatial amplitude or support. Gerchberg–Saxton alternates between planes: propagate, replace the magnitude with the measured value while keeping phase, propagate back, and impose the other constraint.

U → F[U] → measured magnitude + current phase → F−1 → object constraint

Each projection satisfies one condition, but their combination is a nonconvex problem. A small residual does not automatically prove a unique or correct object. Depending on the experiment, shifts, conjugate inversion, or global phase can leave measurements unchanged. Multiple initializations can stagnate at different solutions. Additional distances, known references, or scanning diversity add information rather than merely iterations.

The same loop can design a phase-only hologram by replacing a measured magnitude with a desired output magnitude. That is a synthesis problem: the target may not be exactly reachable under a fixed input amplitude. The notebook reports residuals and compares starts instead of promising exact convergence.
