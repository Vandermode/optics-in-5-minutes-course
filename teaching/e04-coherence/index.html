E04 / WHY LEARN THIS?

Why do fringes disappear when the optics stay put?

An interference pattern can fade after a change in illumination or exposure even when neither path moves. A rough surface can also produce stable speckle. Interpreting these observations requires knowing which relationships between fields survive the camera's averaging.

What you gain: Diagnose changes in fringe visibility, distinguish a static random pattern from lost coherence, and decide when your imaging model should add fields, intensities, or coherent modes.

Unpack the difficult steps

How does a spectral width become a coherence length?

1:08 · Replay this part

Read the film’s lower graph as a delay scan. Its horizontal axis ΔL is the optical path difference between two copies of the same beam; τ = ΔL/c. The fast oscillations are the interference contribution as that path difference changes. The envelope describes fringe visibility. It is not necessarily the shape of a finite pulse moving through space.

For one frequency ν, delaying a copy adds phase 2πντ. For two nearby frequencies separated by δν, the difference between their interference phases grows as 2πδντ. At zero delay their fringe contributions agree; with increasing delay they point in different directions. A continuous band of frequencies can then cancel in the sum, even though every frequency still interferes with its own copy.

δφ = 2π δν τ = 2π δν ΔL/c
g⁽¹⁾(τ) = [∫ Sν(ν)e^(i2πντ) dν] / [∫ Sν(ν) dν]

Sν is power per unit frequency. For a stationary field, its normalized Fourier transform is the complex field autocorrelation g⁽¹⁾ (the Wiener–Khinchin relation, with this sign convention). The visible envelope is |g⁽¹⁾|. It is the complex correlation, not just the real envelope, that forms the Fourier pair with the spectrum.

Δν · τc ~ 1, ℓc = cτc ~ c/Δν
ν = c/λ ⇒ Δν ≈ cΔλ/λ₀² ⇒ ℓc ~ λ₀²/Δλ

The last conversion assumes Δλ ≪ λ₀. Near 550 nm, a 30 nm bandwidth gives the rough scale 550²/30 nm ≈ 10 μm. Reducing the width tenfold makes that scale ten times longer. This is a scale estimate: a precise length needs the spectral shape and a stated width criterion. A two-line spectrum can even show revivals, so total bandwidth alone does not specify every coherence function.

For the Gaussian frequency spectrum in the experiment below, Δν is its full width at half maximum. Define ℓe as the positive path difference at which visibility falls to 1/e. Then ℓe = 0.530 c/Δν; the 550 nm / 30 nm example gives 5.34 μm under this explicit convention. Both this value and the rough 10 μm scale describe the same spectrum using different width conventions.

Sν(ν) ∝ exp[−4 ln 2 · (ν − ν₀)²/Δν²]
|g⁽¹⁾(ΔL/c)| = exp[−(πΔνΔL/c)²/(4 ln 2)]
ℓe = (2√(ln 2)/π) c/Δν

The small experiment assumes equal beam powers, matched polarization, and a common spatial mode, so visibility equals |g⁽¹⁾|. It uses a spectrum Gaussian in frequency; its Δλ label is the narrowband conversion. The full lesson’s bandwidth control instead uses the frequency standard deviation σν, with ΔνFWHM = 2√(2 ln 2) σν.

Do incoherent fields stop adding?

0:34 · Replay this part

⟨|E₁ + E₂|²⟩ = I₁ + I₂ + 2 Re Γ₁₂
Γ₁₂ = ⟨E₁*E₂⟩, g₁₂ = Γ₁₂/√(I₁I₂)
V = [2√(I₁I₂)/(I₁ + I₂)] |g₁₂|

Fields always superpose. The question is whether their cross term survives averaging over the measurement. Uncorrelated relative phases give Γ₁₂ = 0 in the statistical limit, so the measured intensities add. A finite exposure can leave residual fluctuations.

Even perfect coherence does not guarantee unit visibility: unequal beam powers or mismatched polarization reduce contrast. The formula assumes a phase scan with fixed arm intensities and matched polarization. Therefore weak fringes alone do not tell you which kind of coherence was lost.

Why can a pinhole restore fringes without narrowing the spectrum?

1:56 · Replay this part

Δφ(θ) ≈ 2π bθ/λ
gspatial(b) ∝ ∫ B(θ)e^(i2πbθ/λ) dθ

For slit separation b, a small source angle θ gives a relative phase proportional to bθ. Each source point produces a shifted fringe pattern. Summing independent source points averages these different phase offsets. A pinhole reduces the angular spread, making the patterns agree across a larger slit separation, while also discarding power.

This is spatial coherence, a correlation between positions; temporal coherence compares delayed copies. A narrow spectral filter primarily improves the latter, and a small angular source primarily improves the former. For a uniform angular strip of full width Θ, gspatial = sinc(bΘ/λ), with its first zero at bΘ = λ. Other source shapes have different zeros or no finite zero.

How can random phases make a stable speckle pattern?

2:49 · Replay this part

Random across space does not mean fluctuating during exposure. A stationary rough wall illuminated coherently can give each optical path a complicated but fixed phase. Their sum produces a fixed speckle pattern. Averaging repeated copies of that same pattern leaves it unchanged.

C = σI/⟨I⟩, Csum = 1/√N
Neff = (Σ wm)² / Σ wm²

C = 1 is the ideal fully developed, resolved, single-polarization speckle limit. Adding N independent, equal-mean patterns makes the variance grow like N but the mean like N, giving 1/√N. Unequal weights replace N by Neff; correlation, pixel averaging, and polarization change the result. Different colours or source points help only to the extent that they produce different patterns.

Why does diagonalizing mutual intensity let us add mode intensities?

3:29 · Replay this part

J(x₁,x₂) = ⟨U(x₁)U*(x₂)⟩ = Σ wm ψm(x₁)ψm*(x₂)
⟨am an*⟩ = wm δmn
Iout(x) = Σ wm |𝒫ψm(x)|²

J is Hermitian and positive semidefinite, so its eigenvalues wm are nonnegative. Expanding each realization in the orthonormal eigenmodes gives random coefficients whose cross-correlations vanish. After linear propagation 𝒫, that same absence of cross terms lets us add the weighted mode intensities.

Uncorrelated is the property needed here; statistical independence is a stronger claim. A rank-one J needs one spatial mode, which approximates an ideal single-mode laser but not every laser. The number of useful modes depends on the full correlation function and the accuracy required. Broadband propagation may also need wavelength integration.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

Two electric fields superpose whether they are coherent or not. What changes is the detector’s average. For two scalar fields with the same polarization, expanding their squared sum exposes the term responsible for interference.

I = ⟨|U₁ + U₂|²⟩ = I₁ + I₂ + 2 Re⟨U₁U₂*⟩

The brackets describe the variations sampled by the measurement. A relative phase that remains stable can leave a nonzero cross term. Phases that explore a full circle uniformly make that term vanish. Partial averaging gives intermediate contrast. The physical rule remains field superposition; adding intensities is the result of zero cross correlation.

Normalize the correlation as γ = ⟨U₁U₂*⟩/√(I₁I₂). Its magnitude lies between zero and one. Fringe visibility is V = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin). For the scalar model here, V = 2√(I₁I₂)|γ|/(I₁+I₂). Only equally bright, polarization-matched beams give V = |γ|. Poor contrast alone is therefore not enough to diagnose low coherence.

The controls describe several independent averages in a deliberately separable toy model. Phase jitter changes the relative phase during an exposure. Bandwidth and delay compare a wave with a delayed copy. Angular source width averages shifted fringe patterns. The speckle panel is a separate experiment showing which patterns were actually averaged.

By the end, you can

  1. Explain intensity addition as a consequence of averaged cross terms.
  2. Distinguish temporal coherence, spatial coherence, and fixed spatial randomness.
  3. Use mutual intensity and coherent modes to propagate partially coherent light.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

Two kinds of selection, two kinds of correlation.

Temporal coherence compares field samples separated in time. In a stationary source, different frequencies contribute different phase rotations when one interferometer arm is delayed by τ. The normalized field correlation is the Fourier transform of the power spectrum. For a Gaussian frequency distribution of standard deviation σν, its magnitude is exp[−(2πσντ)²/2]. A spectral filter narrows that distribution and lengthens the delay range over which interference survives.

Spatial coherence compares field samples at different positions. Each source point can illuminate both slits and interfere with itself. Independent source points contribute intensities to the camera. Their illumination angles shift the fringes, so an extended source averages shifted copies. For the small-angle, uniform angular strip used here, γ = sinc(Θd/λ), where Θ is its full angular width and sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx).

A pinhole selects angles and usually discards flux; it does not narrow the optical spectrum. A spectral filter selects frequencies; it does not automatically make an extended source spatially coherent. The negative lobes of sinc shift the fringes by π. They are not negative intensities, and the first zero is not a permanent hard cutoff.

Why random phase can make visible speckle.

A stationary rough surface imposes irregular spatial phases. Under stable coherent illumination, those contributions still interfere consistently during an exposure, producing fixed bright and dark grains. Spatial randomness alone does not wash them out. Contrast decreases when the measurement combines different sufficiently independent patterns, polarizations, wavelengths, or unresolved grains.

For resolved, fully developed, single-polarization speckle, contrast C = σI/⟨I⟩ is approximately one. Averaging N independent equal-weight patterns gives C ≈ 1/√N. Repeating one pattern provides no such benefit. A finite image and correlated samples make a measured contrast fluctuate around that ideal prediction.

One matrix carries all pairwise information.

Mutual intensity is Jᵢⱼ = ⟨UᵢUⱼ*⟩. Its diagonal is intensity, but its off-diagonal correlations affect what happens after propagation. J is Hermitian and positive semidefinite. Its eigenvectors are coherent modes and its nonnegative eigenvalues are their powers. Propagate each mode through the same linear field operator and sum its weighted intensity. The notebook checks this against direct independent-source averaging and shows how to measure truncation error.
