E04 / WHY LEARN THIS?

Why do fringes disappear when the optics stay put?

An interference pattern can fade after a change in illumination or exposure even when neither path moves. A rough surface can also produce stable speckle. Interpreting these observations requires knowing which relationships between fields survive the camera's averaging.

What you gain: Diagnose changes in fringe visibility, distinguish a static random pattern from lost coherence, and decide when your imaging model should add fields, intensities, or coherent modes.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

Two electric fields superpose whether they are coherent or not. What changes is the detector’s average. For two scalar fields with the same polarization, expanding their squared sum exposes the term responsible for interference.

I = ⟨|U₁ + U₂|²⟩ = I₁ + I₂ + 2 Re⟨U₁U₂*⟩

The brackets describe the variations sampled by the measurement. A relative phase that remains stable can leave a nonzero cross term. Phases that explore a full circle uniformly make that term vanish. Partial averaging gives intermediate contrast. The physical rule remains field superposition; adding intensities is the result of zero cross correlation.

Normalize the correlation as γ = ⟨U₁U₂*⟩/√(I₁I₂). Its magnitude lies between zero and one. Fringe visibility is V = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin). For the scalar model here, V = 2√(I₁I₂)|γ|/(I₁+I₂). Only equally bright, polarization-matched beams give V = |γ|. Poor contrast alone is therefore not enough to diagnose low coherence.

The controls describe several independent averages in a deliberately separable toy model. Phase jitter changes the relative phase during an exposure. Bandwidth and delay compare a wave with a delayed copy. Angular source width averages shifted fringe patterns. The speckle panel is a separate experiment showing which patterns were actually averaged.

By the end, you can

  1. Explain intensity addition as a consequence of averaged cross terms.
  2. Distinguish temporal coherence, spatial coherence, and fixed spatial randomness.
  3. Use mutual intensity and coherent modes to propagate partially coherent light.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

Two kinds of selection, two kinds of correlation.

Temporal coherence compares field samples separated in time. In a stationary source, different frequencies contribute different phase rotations when one interferometer arm is delayed by τ. The normalized field correlation is the Fourier transform of the power spectrum. For a Gaussian frequency distribution of standard deviation σν, its magnitude is exp[−(2πσντ)²/2]. A spectral filter narrows that distribution and lengthens the delay range over which interference survives.

Spatial coherence compares field samples at different positions. Each source point can illuminate both slits and interfere with itself. Independent source points contribute intensities to the camera. Their illumination angles shift the fringes, so an extended source averages shifted copies. For the small-angle, uniform angular strip used here, γ = sinc(Θd/λ), where Θ is its full angular width and sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx).

A pinhole selects angles and usually discards flux; it does not narrow the optical spectrum. A spectral filter selects frequencies; it does not automatically make an extended source spatially coherent. The negative lobes of sinc shift the fringes by π. They are not negative intensities, and the first zero is not a permanent hard cutoff.

Why random phase can make visible speckle.

A stationary rough surface imposes irregular spatial phases. Under stable coherent illumination, those contributions still interfere consistently during an exposure, producing fixed bright and dark grains. Spatial randomness alone does not wash them out. Contrast decreases when the measurement combines different sufficiently independent patterns, polarizations, wavelengths, or unresolved grains.

For resolved, fully developed, single-polarization speckle, contrast C = σI/⟨I⟩ is approximately one. Averaging N independent equal-weight patterns gives C ≈ 1/√N. Repeating one pattern provides no such benefit. A finite image and correlated samples make a measured contrast fluctuate around that ideal prediction.

One matrix carries all pairwise information.

Mutual intensity is Jᵢⱼ = ⟨UᵢUⱼ*⟩. Its diagonal is intensity, but its off-diagonal correlations affect what happens after propagation. J is Hermitian and positive semidefinite. Its eigenvectors are coherent modes and its nonnegative eigenvalues are their powers. Propagate each mode through the same linear field operator and sum its weighted intensity. The notebook checks this against direct independent-source averaging and shows how to measure truncation error.
