E07 / WHY LEARN THIS?

How does repetition select where light can travel?

Repeated grooves can separate wavelengths into different directions, while repeated layers can form a mirror over a band of wavelengths. Designing either device requires connecting one repeating unit to the waves supported by the whole structure.

What you gain: Use period and angle to find propagating diffraction orders, connect a layer stack to its reflection band, and distinguish a prediction of allowed directions from a prediction of how much power enters each direction.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

A flat interface has no preferred transverse position. Its boundary conditions match the transverse phase variation on both sides, conserving kₓ. Combining kₓ = nk₀ sin θ with that conservation gives Snell’s law. If the transmitted medium has no real longitudinal wavevector for that kₓ, its transmitted field is evanescent.

A periodic interface has a weaker symmetry: it repeats after one period Λ. Its Fourier components occur at integer multiples of K = 2π/Λ. Multiplication by that periodic structure can shift a field’s transverse wavevector by mK, creating diffraction orders.

kₓ,m = kₓ,in + mK   ·   |kₓ,m| < n_out k₀

The second condition selects propagating transmitted orders. Equality is grazing incidence with zero normal flux. The browser uses air on both sides, but the notebook lets you distinguish the incident and exit indices. At normal incidence, higher transmitted orders disappear when Λ < λ₀/n_out; suppressing them on both sides requires considering both media.

This equation predicts directions, not power. Ridge shape, depth, material, and polarization determine how much light enters each allowed order. Equal line brightness in the direction diagram therefore carries no efficiency claim.

By the end, you can

  1. Determine which grating orders propagate in each medium.
  2. Connect a finite Bragg mirror to an infinite crystal’s Bloch gap.
  3. Distinguish energy conservation from convergence in a truncated RCWA calculation.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

One period is an operator.

In a layered medium, a two-component vector holds the tangential electric and magnetic fields. A transfer matrix advances it through one layer. Multiplying layer matrices gives one unit cell, and raising that cell matrix to a power gives a finite stack. Boundary matching then determines reflection and transmission amplitudes.

A Bloch wave returns to the same field shape after one period, multiplied by eiK_BΛ. It is therefore an eigenvector of the unit-cell operator. For a lossless reciprocal two-component stack, the determinant is one, and its eigenvalues occur as reciprocal pairs.

cos(K_BΛ) = ½ tr M_period

If the half trace lies between −1 and 1, K_B is real and the Bloch factor has unit magnitude. Outside that interval, the solutions grow or decay across periods. The decaying solution describes penetration into a semi-infinite stop band. A finite mirror still transmits a small amount, and can show interference fringes outside the gap. Its reflectance is not simply a binary band diagram.

RCWA keeps a finite set of coupled orders.

A layer patterned across x couples Fourier components. Expanding its permittivity produces a matrix whose off-diagonal entries connect different diffraction orders. Diagonalizing that matrix finds the layer’s field modes. Boundary matching then connects incoming, reflected, internal, and transmitted amplitudes.

The optional notebook solves one lamellar layer for TE polarization using forward modes referenced at the entrance and backward modes referenced at the exit. This keeps evanescent propagation factors decaying, avoiding a matrix filled with huge growing exponentials. Power in an order includes its longitudinal wavevector factor; squared amplitude alone is insufficient when angles or refractive indices differ.

A truncated basis can conserve total power while distributing it incorrectly among orders. Increase the retained harmonics and compare individual efficiencies and complex amplitudes, as well as boundary residuals and total R + T. The exercise deliberately shows a low-order calculation with excellent conservation but appreciable diffraction error.
