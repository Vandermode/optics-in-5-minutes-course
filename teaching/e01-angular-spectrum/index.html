E01 / WHY LEARN THIS?

Where will the light go after this plane?

You know the field just after a slit or a phase mask, but the detector sits farther away. Predicting its image means following diffraction and phase through space. The same problem appears when refocusing a hologram or checking whether a designed optic produces its intended pattern.

What you gain: Build a propagation tool that later lessons reuse, predict how aperture, wavelength, and distance change a field, and recognize when a surprising pattern comes from the numerical grid.

Unpack the difficult steps

Why does a Fourier transform turn positions into directions?

2:32 · Replay this part

At z = 0, the function exp(ikx x) is a transverse phase pattern. Extending it into space as exp[i(kx x + kz z)] makes a plane wave. Its wavefront normal points along (kx, kz), so kx/k = sin θ. The transform is therefore a list of complex amplitudes indexed by propagation direction, with additional evanescent components outside |kx| ≤ k.

Ũ(kx) = ∫ U(x,0)e^(−ikx x) dx
U(x,z) = ℱ⁻¹{Ũ(kx)e^(ikz z)}

The coefficients are amplitudes with phases; their squared magnitudes alone cannot reconstruct U. Plane waves are useful because each is an eigenfunction of propagation in a uniform medium, not because the physical beam consists of isolated rays.

Why choose decay, and does every evanescent wave die within one wavelength?

3:35 · Replay this part

kz = iα, α = √(kx² − k²) > 0
e^(ikz z) = e^(−αz), decay length = 1/α

For an outgoing field in the source-free half-space z > 0, choose the branch that stays bounded away from the source. The other branch grows without bound. The decay length depends on kx: just above the cutoff it can be much longer than λ; far above it, much shorter. The film’s wavelength-scale description is a useful scale estimate, not a universal bound.

The sampling condition also depends on the frequencies you need. To represent transverse frequencies up to kx,max, require Δx < π/kx,max. Resolving evanescent detail can therefore demand spacing finer than λ/2.

Why do padding and propagation distance matter if the formula is exact?

4:24 · Replay this part

A discrete Fourier transform treats the sampled window as one period of an endlessly repeated field. A diffracting beam that leaves one edge can reappear at the opposite edge. Padding gives the beam more empty space before those periodic copies overlap; it does not restore frequencies lost by coarse sampling.

Longer propagation also makes the transfer-function phase vary more rapidly across frequency samples. Check convergence by enlarging the window and refining the grid independently. The paraxial sampling crossover discussed in E02 is a guide for choosing a propagator, not proof that every angular-spectrum computation below it is accurate.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

A slit removes part of an incident wave. Immediately after the opening, the field is bright in a narrow strip. Farther away, bright and dark bands appear outside that strip. Straight rays alone cannot explain the pattern: contributions from different parts of the opening interfere.

Write one monochromatic field as U(x) = A(x)eiφ(x). A complex number is an arrow: its length is amplitude, its angle is phase. The physical electric field is the real part of this slowly described amplitude multiplied by e−iωt. A camera averages the rapid oscillation and records a quantity proportional to |U|². Consequently, equal intensities do not imply equal fields.

A plane wave has a particularly simple transverse profile, eikₓx. Its phase increases by kₓ radians per unit distance in x. A Fourier transform tells us how much of each such profile is present. These coefficients are complex amplitudes, so we must retain their phases when rebuilding the field.

k = 2π/λ   ·   kₓ = 2πfₓ   ·   kₓ² + k_z² = k²

The last relation is the scalar Helmholtz equation evaluated on a plane wave. It links a transverse frequency to a longitudinal propagation constant. For a propagating component, sin θ = kₓ/k. Thus a spatial-frequency plot is also a map of propagation directions.

By the end, you can

  1. Interpret a Fourier coefficient as a plane-wave amplitude.
  2. Predict how wavelength, aperture width, and distance change diffraction.
  3. Separate physical evanescent decay from numerical aliasing and wrap-around.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

Propagation becomes multiplication.

In homogeneous space, the plane-wave components evolve independently. Moving forward by z multiplies one coefficient by H = eik_zz. The spatial field changes shape because different directions acquire different phases, even though each individual plane wave keeps its shape.

U(x,z) = F−1{ F[U(x,0)] · eiz√(k²−kₓ²) }

When |kₓ| exceeds k, take k_z = i√(kₓ²−k²), choosing the branch that decays away from the source. The multiplier is then a decreasing real exponential. This is an evanescent component, not a propagating wave that has been stopped by an aperture. Recovering it by backward exponential amplification would be extremely sensitive to noise.

For small angles, expand the square root: k_z ≈ k − kₓ²/(2k). That is the Fresnel approximation. It can be excellent for narrow angular spectra but does not represent the evanescent branch. Switching the model is a physical approximation; increasing the grid size is a numerical refinement. The notebook lets you vary these independently.

The FFT adds an implicit boundary condition.

A discrete FFT describes a periodically repeated window. If the diffracted field reaches the edge, it can re-enter from the opposite side. Zero padding separates the copies. Padding does not create higher spatial resolution: the sample spacing still sets the Nyquist frequency. To include the entire propagating band at normal scalar sampling, Δx ≤ λ/2 is sufficient; evanescent structure can require still smaller spacing.

For a spectrum containing only propagating components, |H| = 1, so the discrete field norm is conserved. A slit also contains evanescent frequencies, whose squared amplitudes decay. The power ratio on this page refers to the complete FFT grid, not just the visible central crop, and is an intensity norm rather than a rigorous vector-field flux measurement.
