E09 / WHY LEARN THIS?

When can you trust a simulated electromagnetic field?

A device with reflections, interfaces, or wavelength-scale features may need Maxwell's coupled fields rather than a prescribed phase mask. Computing those fields still does not make every plotted ripple physical: the grid, time step, and artificial boundaries can change the result.

What you gain: Build a time-domain Maxwell solver and test its stability, propagation error, and boundary reflections before interpreting a simulated device response.

Unpack the difficult steps

Why move H half a cell and half a time step away from E?

1:15 · Replay this part

∂E/∂x |x+Δx/2 ≈ [E(x+Δx) − E(x)]/Δx

The difference of two neighbouring E samples estimates a derivative at their midpoint. Storing the relevant H component there aligns the Maxwell update with that centred derivative. In time, E at step n updates H from n − 1/2 to n + 1/2; that new H then updates E from n to n + 1. Neither update needs a simultaneous global solve in the basic explicit scheme.

This structure gives second-order differences for smooth fields on the standard grid. Interfaces, curved boundaries, dispersive materials, and source placement need additional care. A single scalar ε per cell describes the simple nondispersive material model in the film, not every electromagnetic material.

Why can a simulation be stable and still give the wrong wave speed?

1:56 · Replay this part

Δt ≤ 1 / [vmax √(Δx⁻² + Δy⁻² + Δz⁻²)]
2D square grid in vacuum: cΔt/Δx ≤ 1/√2

For the standard explicit Yee scheme in a simple lossless medium, the Courant bound keeps the discrete update’s wave modes from growing spuriously. Use only the active dimensions in the formula. The film’s circle-inside-a-diamond picture gives intuition; the numerical stability condition is a property of the update equations.

Below that bound, finite differences still have a dispersion relation different from Maxwell’s continuous one. Phase speed depends on wavelength and direction. Resolve the shortest relevant wavelength inside the highest-index material, then refine further until the observable converges. Ten to twenty cells per wavelength is a starting estimate, not an accuracy certificate; resonances and small gaps often demand more.

How can an absorbing region avoid reflecting at its entrance?

3:12 · Replay this part

x̃ = x + (i/ω)∫ σ(s) ds
e^(ikx̃) = e^(ikx) exp[−(k/ω)∫ σ(s) ds]

With the chosen time convention, a positive imaginary coordinate stretch makes an outgoing plane wave decay. The transformed Maxwell equations include matched electric and magnetic responses, so their ideal continuum interface retains the original wave impedance. Simply adding electric loss to an ordinary material does not make that same match.

The equation above explains the mechanism for the simple outgoing-wave case. A finite discretized PML is not reflection-free for every problem. Use a gradual profile, enough thickness, and space between the structure and absorber; repeat with changed PML settings. Grazing incidence, evanescent fields, and some inhomogeneous or backward-wave media need particular care.

How can one short pulse give many wavelengths, and what limits the result?

4:09 · Replay this part

linear response: Eout(ω) = H(ω) Ein(ω)
frequency resolution scale: Δf ~ 1/Trecord

In a linear time-invariant system, different frequencies respond independently. A pulse probes the frequencies where its source spectrum has useful power. Transform the measured fields in time and normalize to the incident spectrum; transmission and reflection require power flux or mode amplitudes, not an arbitrary single field sample.

Finite recording time broadens spectral features and can truncate a resonator’s long tail. A very narrow resonance needs a long run even if the excitation is brief. Check source bandwidth, monitor definitions, residual stored energy, and record-length convergence before trusting a spectrum.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

Maxwell’s equations couple electric and magnetic fields through spatial derivatives. In one dimension, with E along z and propagation along x, the magnetic field lies along y. In normalized units, the two update equations connect neighboring samples rather than solving for an entire field at once.

Hᵧ ← Hᵧ + S ΔₓE_z   ·   E_z ← E_z + (S/ε) ΔₓHᵧ

Here S = c₀Δt/Δx is the Courant number, ε is relative permittivity, and Δₓ means a difference between adjacent samples without division by Δx. The electric field is stored at integer positions and times. The magnetic field is half a cell away and half a time step behind. Updating H first and E second advances them in a leapfrog.

Staggering is not a plotting inconvenience. The difference of two values approximates a derivative at their midpoint, so the other field naturally belongs there. The same idea applies in time. For a physical wave traveling in +x with these axis conventions, Hᵧ = −nE_z; opposite signs do not mean opposite propagation directions.

The browser computes a real 1D finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulation on a Yee grid. A soft electric source launches pulses both left and right. The left edge always absorbs. The right edge can be a wall, an abrupt electric-loss layer, or a matched graded CPML. A dielectric interface can be inserted at x = 8.

By the end, you can

  1. Explain the spatial and temporal staggering of a Yee update.
  2. Distinguish instability, numerical dispersion, and boundary reflection.
  3. Measure reflection with a reference run and verify an absorber by refinement.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

The grid has its own limits.

For the vacuum 1D Yee scheme used here, stability requires S ≤ 1. In equal-grid 2D, the corresponding limit is S ≤ 1/√2. These are different bounds: a value that is safe in one dimension can be unstable in two. The notebook labels dimensionality explicitly and seeds a tiny alternating perturbation so an unstable mode becomes visible reproducibly.

Stability is only the first check. A stable grid can give the wrong phase velocity because finite differences have a different dispersion relation from continuous derivatives. Coarse pixels accumulate phase error as the wave travels. Cells must be counted per wavelength inside the highest-index material, not just per vacuum wavelength.

sin²(ωΔt/2) = (S²/n²) Σ_d sin²(k_dΔx/2)

The sum has one term in the browser and two in the optional 2D calculation. Increasing resolution should move numerical results toward the continuum answer. A small-looking pixel size alone is not evidence of convergence.

Absorption and matching solve different problems.

An abrupt electric conductivity dissipates energy but changes wave impedance, so its entrance reflects. A perfectly matched layer modifies the derivative operators in a coordinated way. In the continuum idealization its entrance is matched while waves attenuate inside. CPML realizes the frequency-dependent stretch with memory variables in the time-domain update.

The discrete implementation still has a finite reflection error. Its electric and magnetic loss profiles must be sampled at their actual staggered positions. Grading, layer thickness, resolution, and incidence conditions affect the residual. Calling a region PML is not a substitute for measuring its echo.

The notebook subtracts a larger-domain reference field from a test trace, then gates the reflected arrival. It also compares the dielectric step with Fresnel reflectance. For precise power balance, electric and magnetic fields must be colocated in both space and time before calculating flux; simply squaring an arbitrary field sample can hide staggering error.
