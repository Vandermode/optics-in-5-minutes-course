E03 / WHY LEARN THIS?

Why does more magnification stop revealing detail?

You want to distinguish two nearby emitters in a microscope. Enlarging the image also enlarges their blur spots, so a bigger picture alone does not tell you whether the optics captured the separation. You need a way to connect the instrument to the information it records.

What you gain: Compare changes in numerical aperture and wavelength, distinguish optical resolution from display zoom, and judge which detail a measured image can support before attempting deconvolution.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

Every point leaves a footprint.

A point source is imaged as an Airy pattern: a bright central spot surrounded by faint rings. This is the lens’s point spread function, or PSF.

For independent glowing points, like fluorescent emitters, the intensities add. An extended object is a sum of these footprints: an intensity convolution, I = O ∗ h.

By the end, you can

  1. Predict how numerical aperture and wavelength change the PSF.
  2. Distinguish a two-point resolution criterion from a spatial-frequency cutoff.
  3. Explain why magnification and deconvolution cannot create missing measurements.

03 / NAME THE LIMIT

One lens. Two different questions.

TWO POINTS

How much do their images overlap?

dR ≈ 0.61 λ0 / NA

At the Rayleigh separation, the central peak of one Airy pattern lies at the first zero of the other. This convention applies here to two equally bright, mutually incoherent points.

A PERIODIC PATTERN

Which frequencies reach the image?

fc = 2 NA / λ0

The incoherent intensity transfer function reaches zero at this cutoff. Its reciprocal, λ0/(2 NA), is a limiting period—not the Rayleigh two-point distance. Contrast is already zero at the cutoff.

Where do the Airy pattern and the factor 0.61 come from?

For a circular pupil, the peak-normalized intensity PSF is

h(r) = [2 J₁(v) / v]²,   v = 2π NA r / λ0.

J₁ is the first-order Bessel function, and h(0) = 1 by its limit. Its first positive zero is v ≈ 3.831706. Dividing by 2π gives 0.609835. The two-point image is h(√((x − d/2)² + y²)) + h(√((x + d/2)² + y²)).

For an incoherent sinusoidal object, the modulation transfer function (MTF) tells you what fraction of the input contrast survives.

Only part of this pattern’s contrast survives.

At zero frequency the pattern is uniform; “contrast transmitted” denotes the transfer function’s DC limit.

Both strips span 4 μm and use the same linear gray scale. This example is an infinite sinusoid, not a finite cropped object.

See the transfer function and the coherent-imaging distinction

For this ideal circular pupil, H(ρ) = (2/π)[arccos ρ − ρ√(1 − ρ²)] for 0 ≤ ρ < 1, and H = 0 for ρ ≥ 1, where ρ = |f|/fc. H is real and nonnegative, so MTF = |H| = H.

For coherent illumination, the pupil instead filters the complex field with cutoff NA/λ0; intensity is taken afterward. Do not compare that field cutoff to an intensity cutoff without specifying the object and illumination. This experiment stays with incoherent emitters throughout.

# An incoherent image is intensity convolution.
field_psf = np.fft.ifft2(pupil)
psf = np.abs(field_psf) ** 2
psf /= psf.sum()

otf = np.fft.fft2(psf)
image = np.fft.ifft2(
    np.fft.fft2(object_intensity) * otf
).real

MODEL, MATERIALS & SOURCES

Know what the model leaves out.

This is a scalar, monochromatic, shift-invariant model. It omits aberrations, vector polarization effects, defocus, finite emitters, detector pixel integration, and photon noise in the browser demo. High-NA behavior is illustrative; vector models may be needed for quantitative microscopy. The notebook introduces additive noise separately.
