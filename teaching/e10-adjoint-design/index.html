E10 / WHY LEARN THIS?

How do you improve a design with thousands of controls?

You want a phase mask to produce a chosen intensity pattern, but each pixel is a design variable. Perturbing every pixel separately makes each optimization step expensive. You need the sensitivity of one objective to all those variables at once.

What you gain: Compute and verify a gradient with an adjoint calculation, use it to improve the pattern, and assess the resulting design without assuming that a lower error proves a global optimum.

Unpack the difficult steps

Where do the thousands of solves disappear?

1:23 · Replay this part

A x = b ⇒ A (∂x/∂pj) = ∂b/∂pj − (∂A/∂pj)x
real-variable form: Aᵀλ = ∂F/∂x
dF/dpj = ∂F/∂pj + λᵀ[∂b/∂pj − (∂A/∂pj)x]

A direct sensitivity method solves for ∂x/∂pj separately for every parameter. The adjoint instead solves for one λ that represents how the scalar objective responds to the entire field. Once x and λ are known, each parameter derivative needs only the corresponding contraction. Local material parameters make those contractions especially cheap.

Two solves means one forward plus one adjoint for one scalar objective and one source/frequency state of a linear field problem. Multiple independent states generally need additional forward/adjoint work, and every optimization iteration repeats it. The cheap contractions and parameter filtering still scale with design size.

Does “send light backward” literally mean reversing the simulation?

1:57 · Replay this part

complex convention: δF = 2 Re(q†δx) + explicit parameter terms
A†λ = q
dF/dpj = explicit term + 2 Re{λ†[∂b/∂pj − (∂A/∂pj)x]}

For complex fields and a real objective, the derivative convention must be stated. The Hermitian-adjoint form above uses A†. An equivalent derivation can use a transpose solve after conjugating the adjoint variable and source appropriately. The film’s Aᵀ picture assumes a reciprocal, suitably represented operator; symmetry is not a statement that A is real or Hermitian.

The adjoint source is weighted by the objective derivative, not simply an identical source placed at every output. Loss, nonreciprocity, absorbing boundaries, and discretization make a literal time-reversal story insufficient. What must hold is the discrete adjoint equation for the exact forward computation being differentiated.

When is the adjoint propagator different from backward propagation?

2:59 · Replay this part

propagating: H(z) = e^(ikz z), H* = H(−z) = H⁻¹
evanescent: H(z) = e^(−αz), H* = e^(−αz) ≠ H⁻¹ = e^(+αz)

For real kz in a lossless medium, H has unit magnitude, so conjugating it is equivalent to reversing z. For an evanescent component, the adjoint retains the decay; the mathematical inverse would amplify it. Aperture clipping and loss also make the adjoint different from an inverse. A sensitivity backpass does not recover information erased by the forward model.

Likewise, the adjoint of a discrete FFT includes normalization factors unless the transform is defined to be unitary. Automatic differentiation tracks the actual implementation, including those factors. Check a directional derivative against finite differences before trusting a custom backward operator.

If the score improves, why might the manufactured design still fail?

4:16 · Replay this part

An optimizer finds opportunities in the model it sees. Intermediate material values, sub-grid features, omitted vector effects, a narrow training wavelength, or an unrealistic reconstruction noise model can all improve the simulated score without improving the real device. A good gradient cannot correct an incomplete objective or forward model.

Parameterize or constrain manufacturable structures, include relevant wavelengths, sources, depths, and noise in the objective, and validate the final geometry with refined settings or an independent solver. Compare against the same baseline and operating conditions. Gradient-based improvement is local; it does not certify a global optimum.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

Forward modeling answers what a device does. Design asks which parameters make it do something useful. Here the parameters are the phase delays of a fixed-amplitude pupil. A unitary Fourier transform produces the output field, and its intensity is compared with a target.

u = a eiφ   ·   v = Fu   ·   J = ½ Σ(|v|² − T)² / ΣT²

The aperture a and target T are normalized once so their total powers agree. The loss is an intensity mismatch, not an amplitude mismatch. A central finite difference would perturb each φ separately in both directions, costing roughly twice as many forward evaluations as there are parameters.

Reverse differentiation shares the effect of the final error. First compute the output field v and residual r = |v|² − T. Weight that field by the residual and propagate it through the Hermitian adjoint F†. One overlap with the input field then gives every real phase derivative.

b = F†[rv / ΣT²]   ·   ∂J/∂φ = 2 Im(u* b)

The complex conjugate and the factor of two are essential. The notebook derives this expression from dJ = 2 Re(q†dv), then compares it with numerical perturbations. A visually plausible sensitivity map is not a gradient check.

By the end, you can

  1. Derive a real phase gradient through a complex Fourier operator.
  2. Verify that gradient with central finite differences.
  3. Explain why adjoints are not generally inverse propagation and why gradients do not guarantee global optimality.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

The adjoint follows the computation backward.

For a linear field operator P, the adjoint P† is defined by the complex inner product: ⟨Px,y⟩ = ⟨x,P†y⟩. It transports sensitivity to a scalar objective back through the model. For a unitary Fourier transform, the adjoint equals the inverse, which makes this example especially simple.

That equality is not general. If propagation is diagonal in a spectral basis with factors H, the adjoint uses H*, whereas an inverse uses 1/H. Evanescent attenuation remains attenuation under the adjoint; inverse propagation would amplify it. Lossy systems therefore make a useful counterexample to the phrase run the light backward.

The same idea applies to a matrix wave solver.

Suppose a discretized frequency-domain model is A(p)x = b. A real scalar objective gives an output sensitivity q = ∂J/∂x*. Solve A†λ = q. If b has no parameter dependence, each real parameter derivative is −2 Re[λ†(∂A/∂p)x], plus any explicit objective dependence.

The optional sparse Helmholtz example uses precisely this Hermitian convention and checks all pixel derivatives. An equivalent transpose convention can be used if its source and overlap are conjugated consistently. Mixing the two conventions is a common source of gradients with convincing structure and incorrect signs.

Accurate derivatives are the beginning of design.

The browser uses a seeded initial phase and backtracking descent. Every accepted step must lower the stated loss. Computing the gradient needs one forward and one adjoint operation per scalar objective, while line search adds forward trials. The counter reports those additional evaluations rather than hiding them in a two-simulation claim.

The objective is nonconvex. Multiple starts can end at different local solutions. A phase pattern that works in the numerical model can also fail after quantization, smoothing, or a more accurate wave model. The notebook applies a fabrication projection and evaluates the resulting device again. A lower training loss is useful evidence, but it is not a certificate of global optimality or experimental performance.
