01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

A plane wave is a direction with a phase. A finite lens collects only a limited patch of that wave, so it produces a finite focal spot rather than a mathematical point. Change the wave’s direction and the spot moves. The focal plane therefore turns an angular spectrum into a spatial pattern you can inspect.

x_f = λf fₓ ≈ f sin θ

Here f is focal length, fₓ is transverse spatial frequency in cycles per unit length, and x_f is position in the focal plane. This relation belongs to the paraxial Fourier model. Doubling f doubles the spot displacement and also broadens the diffraction scale of a fixed entrance aperture. A larger picture in that plane does not create new information.

Why a Fourier transform? Free propagation contains a quadratic phase, often called a chirp. A thin paraxial lens supplies the opposite quadratic phase. At the focal distance, one quadratic term cancels and the remaining cross term is the Fourier kernel. Placing the object in the front focal plane removes an additional coordinate-dependent phase, giving the standard front-to-back focal-plane transform.

This page separates two demonstrations. The upper curve has physical wavelength, focal length, and tilt units. The sampled letter experiment below uses dimensionless pixel coordinates so that you can inspect Fourier filtering without confusing image sampling with magnification.

By the end, you can

  1. Convert transverse spatial frequency into a focal-plane coordinate.
  2. Explain why two Fourier transforms invert an image.
  3. Predict how Fourier-plane masks change amplitude and phase objects.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

Filter the field before you square it.

A 4f system has two lenses and a shared Fourier plane. The first lens produces the object’s complex spectrum. A mask multiplies that spectrum. The second lens applies another forward Fourier transform, returning an inverted spatial field. The camera finally records its squared magnitude.

U_out ∝ F{ M · F[U_in] }   ·   I_out = |U_out|²

With no mask, two transforms map x to −x, apart from coordinate scaling and overall phase. A low-pass mask keeps only slowly varying field components and spreads sharp edges. A central stop removes low frequencies, including much of the smooth background. The remaining field often emphasizes changes, but its recorded intensity is not simply the signed derivative of the original picture.

A transparent object can still change light.

A thin phase object multiplies the field by eiφ(x,y). Its intensity may be uniform even when φ varies. For a weak object, eiφ ≈ 1 + iφ: an unscattered background and a small scattered contribution are approximately a quarter-cycle apart. Their first-order intensity contrast is then small.

A small phase disk near the Fourier origin changes the phase of the background relative to the scattered field. Their interference can turn phase variation into visible intensity contrast. The disk does not read an absolute optical phase; it creates a relative-phase measurement. Its finite size also affects low-frequency scattered components, so the resulting image is not an exact quantitative phase map.

Coordinates are part of the algorithm.

For an N-point input sampled at Δx, Fourier bins are separated by Δfₓ = 1/(NΔx). In the focal plane, Δx_f = λf/(NΔx). Relabeling the FFT with the input coordinates is a common mistake. The notebook checks this scaling and the reversal explicitly, then examines how quadratic phases can be undersampled.
